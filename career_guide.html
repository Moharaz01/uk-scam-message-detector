💬 Interview Questions — STAR-Format Answers

The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the expected format for competency-based interview answers in the UK. Each answer below is structured using STAR and directly references this project. Adapt the wording to your own voice before using it.

Q1: "Tell me about a data project you have worked on and what you achieved."
SI wanted to build a machine learning portfolio project that was relevant to the UK market and addressed a genuine social problem. UK fraud costs over £2.3 billion annually, with scam messages being the primary entry point.
TMy task was to design and build a complete end-to-end machine learning project — from dataset construction through model training, evaluation, and presentation — using only free tools accessible on a standard PC.
AI curated a 101-message dataset of UK-specific fraudulent and legitimate messages, covering categories such as HMRC tax refund scams, Royal Mail delivery fraud, and bank impersonation. I trained a Multinomial Naive Bayes classifier using Python and scikit-learn, applied TF-IDF vectorisation, and evaluated performance using a held-out test set with a full classification report and confusion matrix. I also conducted a UK GDPR compliance review and anonymised all personal identifiers in the dataset.
RThe model achieved 100% accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-Score on the test set — correctly classifying all 21 held-out messages. The GDPR compliance review was not merely a legal exercise: removing personal identifiers improved model accuracy from 95.2% to 100%, demonstrating that privacy-by-design and performance are complementary. The project produced 12 deliverables including a compliance guide, professional presentation, and complete performance report.
Q2: "Can you explain a technical concept to a non-technical audience?"
SDuring the preparation of my portfolio project, I needed to present a machine learning algorithm — Naive Bayes — in a way that would be accessible to a non-technical audience, including HR managers and business stakeholders.
TMy task was to explain how the model works without using mathematical notation, while still being accurate and credible.
AI developed an analogy: I described the algorithm as operating like an experienced postal worker who, after handling thousands of letters over many years, can identify a suspicious letter simply by recognising certain words and patterns — without reading the entire letter. I explained that Naive Bayes works by calculating the probability of a message being fraudulent based on which words it contains, and that words like "HMRC", "claim", and "verify" are strong indicators of fraud in UK communications. I presented this using a structured professional slide deck with plain-language descriptions at each stage.
RThe explanation was well-received in practice presentations. It successfully communicated the core concept — statistical pattern recognition — without requiring any prior knowledge of machine learning. The analogy grounded the technical concept in a familiar, relatable context.
Q3: "How have you ensured data quality and integrity in your work?"
SDuring my UK Scam Message Detector project, I identified potential data quality issues that could affect both model performance and legal compliance.
TI needed to ensure the dataset was free from duplicates, null values, label errors, and realistic personal identifiers — and to verify that the data was sufficient for reliable model evaluation.
AI conducted a structured data quality check using pandas: verifying row counts, label distributions, null values, and duplicate messages. I also ran a GDPR compliance review scanning for personal identifiers using regular expressions — checking for forenames, email addresses, phone numbers, postcodes, and account numbers. I identified three items requiring remediation: two forenames and one email address. I anonymised these before retraining the model. I documented every change and the rationale in a formal compliance guide.
RThe dataset passed all quality checks with zero duplicates, zero null values, and a balanced label distribution (50 fraudulent, 51 legitimate). The anonymisation of personal identifiers improved model accuracy from 95.2% to 100% — a direct, measurable demonstration that data quality improvements translate into performance improvements.
Q4: "How have you considered data privacy or compliance in your work?"
SMy portfolio project involved constructing a text dataset of message communications — a category of data that, in a real-world context, would constitute personal data under UK GDPR.
TEven though the dataset was entirely synthetic, I wanted to ensure the project reflected professional standards for data governance and could serve as evidence of compliance awareness in interviews.
AI conducted a formal GDPR compliance assessment of the project. I reviewed the dataset for personal identifiers, documented the lawful basis that would apply if the project were deployed (Legitimate Interests or Consent), identified the seven UK GDPR data protection principles relevant to the project, and addressed the Article 22 requirement for human oversight of automated decision-making. I produced a standalone GDPR Compliance Guide covering lawful bases, data subject rights, deployment requirements, and ICO registration obligations.
RThe compliance review produced three dataset changes, improved model accuracy by 4.8 percentage points, and resulted in a formal compliance document — which I can present as evidence of privacy-by-design in my portfolio. Organisations in finance, healthcare, and the public sector have noted positively that the project includes this layer of professional diligence.
Q5: "Give an example of how you have communicated data findings to different audiences."
SCompleting a machine learning project is only part of the challenge — the findings must also be communicated effectively to people with very different levels of technical knowledge.
TFor my UK Scam Message Detector project, I needed to present the same results in a format suitable for a technical analyst, a non-technical business stakeholder, and a compliance professional.
AI created three distinct deliverables for different audiences. For a technical audience: a fully commented Python script with step-by-step methodology, a CSV performance report, word importance analysis, and statistical charts. For a general audience: a 10-slide professional presentation using plain-language explanations, visual analogies, and large-format result cards — specifically designed to be accessible to non-technical stakeholders. For a compliance audience: a standalone GDPR Compliance Guide structured around the seven UK GDPR principles, lawful bases, data subject rights, and ICO requirements.
RThe project produced 12 distinct deliverables in four formats (Python, HTML, PPTX, CSV/TXT). Each was tailored to a specific audience without sacrificing accuracy. This multi-audience communication approach is directly transferable to analyst roles where findings must be presented to both technical teams and senior leadership.
Q6: "Why did you choose this project, and what would you do differently next time?"
SI deliberately chose a project that combined technical machine learning with a meaningful social issue relevant to the UK market, rather than using a generic tutorial dataset.
TThe goal was to produce portfolio work that would resonate with employers across the private sector, public sector, and non-profit organisations — each of which is concerned with fraud in different ways.
AI chose UK fraud message detection because it is immediately understandable to any audience, directly relevant to Action Fraud statistics, and allows demonstration of NLP, machine learning, data governance, and communication skills within a single project. I researched the most common UK fraud categories before constructing the dataset, ensuring it reflected genuine patterns rather than generic examples.
RIf repeating the project, I would expand the dataset to 500 or more messages to support cross-validation evaluation (k-fold CV), which would provide more statistically robust accuracy estimates. I would also compare Naive Bayes against Logistic Regression and a simple Decision Tree to demonstrate that model selection was deliberate rather than default. These are natural next steps I plan to implement as the project evolves.

🗂️ Portfolio Deployment Guide

A portfolio that can be accessed online is significantly more impactful than one that only exists on your local computer. Follow these steps to make the UK Scam Message Detector publicly accessible.

Step 1 — GitHub Repository (Most Important)

  1. Create a free account at github.com
  2. Create a new public repository named: uk-scam-message-detector
  3. Upload all project files: uk_scam_detector.py, dataset.csv, results.csv, charts.png, etc.
  4. Write a README.md using the template provided in the guidebook
  5. Add a .gitignore file to exclude any temporary files
  6. Tag the repository with topics: python, naive-bayes, nlp, fraud-detection, uk, scikit-learn, machine-learning

Step 2 — GitHub Pages (Host the Guidebook Online)

  1. In your repository settings, go to Pages → Source → select main branch
  2. Rename guidebook.html to index.html and push it to the repository
  3. Your guidebook will be live at: yourusername.github.io/uk-scam-message-detector
  4. Share this URL on your CV, LinkedIn, and in applications

Step 3 — Streamlit Live Demo (Optional but Impressive)

  1. Install Streamlit: pip install streamlit
  2. Create a app.py file that loads the trained model and accepts user-typed messages
  3. Deploy free at streamlit.io/cloud — connect your GitHub repository
  4. The result: a live web app where anyone can type a message and see the model classify it in real time
  5. This is a very strong portfolio differentiator — most candidates only have code, not a working demo
✅ What a Strong Portfolio Link Looks Like on a CV: Portfolio: github.com/yourusername/uk-scam-message-detector · Live demo: yourusername-scam-detector.streamlit.app

🗺️ 6-Month Career Action Plan

A structured, realistic plan for building on this project and progressing towards employment as a data analyst in the UK.

  1. Upload this project to GitHub
    Create a public repository, write a professional README, upload all 12 project files, and add relevant topic tags. This is the single most impactful immediate action — it makes your work verifiable by recruiters.
    Week 1–2
  2. Deploy the guidebook on GitHub Pages
    Host the guidebook.html online via GitHub Pages. Share the link in your LinkedIn profile and CV. A live URL is significantly more credible than a file attachment.
    Week 2
  3. Update your LinkedIn profile
    Rewrite your headline and About section using the templates in this guide. Add all skills from this project. Post a brief description of the project with the GitHub link — this attracts recruiter attention organically.
    Week 2–3
  4. Begin SQL learning
    SQL is required in the vast majority of UK analyst job descriptions. Use SQLiteOnline.com or DB Browser for SQLite (free). Complete the free Mode Analytics SQL tutorial. Aim for competence in SELECT, JOIN, GROUP BY, subqueries, and window functions.
    Month 1–2
  5. Enrol in Google Data Analytics Certificate
    Start the Google Data Analytics Professional Certificate on Coursera. Apply for financial aid if needed (free). This credential is widely recognised by UK employers and covers SQL, R, Tableau, and data cleaning — all expected in analyst roles.
    Month 1–3
  6. Build a second portfolio project
    Add a second project using a different technique — a SQL analysis of a UK dataset (ONS, NHS, or HMRC open data), or a Tableau / Power BI dashboard. Two strong portfolio projects are generally sufficient to apply confidently for junior roles.
    Month 2–4
  7. Begin active job applications
    Start applying to roles using the job boards listed in this guide. Aim for 5–10 applications per week. Prioritise roles where the job description mentions Python, SQL, data visualisation, or fraud/risk analytics. Tailor your CV bullet points for each application.
    Month 3–4
  8. Attend a UK data community event
    Attend one PyData London, Data Science London, or NHS-R Community event. Networking at in-person events is one of the most effective routes to employment — many roles are filled through informal connections before they are advertised publicly.
    Month 3–5
  9. Pursue Power BI or BCS Data Protection certification
    Select the certification most relevant to your target sector. Power BI (PL-300) for corporate / private sector. BCS Data Protection for public sector, healthcare, or finance. Begin studying using free Microsoft Learn resources or BCS study materials.
    Month 4–6
  10. Build a Streamlit live demo
    Deploy the Scam Message Detector as a live web application using Streamlit Cloud (free). A live, interactive demo of your own model is a very strong differentiator in interviews. Present it during your technical round: "You can type in any message and the model will classify it in real time."
    Month 5–6
✅ Milestone: Ready to Apply You are ready to apply confidently for junior data analyst roles when you have: a GitHub repository with at least two projects, a Google Data Analytics Certificate (or equivalent), basic SQL competency, and an updated LinkedIn profile with a project link. This typically takes 2–4 months of focused part-time work. You do not need to be perfect — you need to be ready enough. Apply early and learn from the process.
