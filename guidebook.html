📌 1. What is this project?

This project develops an AI-powered fraud message classifier specifically trained on UK-context messages. It reads any SMS or email message and decides: is this a SCAM or is it LEGITIMATE?

📬
Think of it like this:

Consider the experience of a postal worker who has processed thousands of letters over many years. Through experience, suspicious letters become immediately recognisable — phrases like "you've WON!" or "urgent legal action" set off alarm bells immediately. Certain recurring patterns make identification straightforward without reading every word.

Naive Bayes applies exactly this principle. It learns which words appear in scam messages, and which words appear in normal messages. When it sees a new message, it calculates: "Based on the words I see, how likely is this to be a scam?"

In the UK, fraudulent messages commonly impersonate:

  • 🏛️ HMRC — fake tax refunds or legal threats
  • 📦 Royal Mail / Evri / DPD — fraudulent parcel delivery fees
  • 🏥 NHS — fake appointment links
  • 🏦 Banks (Barclays, Lloyds, Halifax) — fake fraud alerts
  • 🚗 DVLA — fake vehicle tax warnings
  • 💰 PPI companies — compensation scams

Action Fraud UK reported over £2.3 billion lost to fraud in 2023, making this a critically important real-world tool.

🎯 2. Why Naive Bayes? Why UK Scams?

Why Naive Bayes?

✅ Advantages of Naive Bayes for this task: It's extremely fast to train (milliseconds), works well even with small datasets, is very good at text classification, and is easy to understand and explain. The algorithm was invented by Thomas Bayes — a British statistician from Tunbridge Wells — making it a fitting choice for a UK project!

Why UK Scams?

Scam detection is one of the most practical and impactful applications of machine learning. The UK market has very specific scam patterns that make it an interesting dataset — HMRC references, Royal Mail, NHS, pound signs (£), specific UK phone number formats (0800, 0203, 07xxx), and UK-specific brands.

📊 UK Fraud Statistics (2023-2024): UK Finance reported 3.2 million cases of fraud in 2023. Remote purchase fraud was the most common type. The average victim loses £590 per fraud incident. Text message fraud (commonly referred to as smishing (SMS phishing)) increased by 67% year-on-year.

🧠 3. How Naive Bayes Works — An Accessible Explanation

Let's break this down without any maths jargon.

Step 1: Learning Phase (Training)

📚
The studying phase:

We show the model 80 messages we already know are SCAM or LEGITIMATE. The model reads through all of them and makes two lists:

Scam word frequency: "claim" appeared 18 times in scam messages, "refund" appeared 12 times...
Legit word frequency: "appointment" appeared 8 times in legit messages, "delivery" appeared 9 times...

Step 2: Probability Calculation

Naive Bayes calculates a probability for each word. For example:

  • The word "HMRC" appears in 60% of scam messages but only 2% of legit ones → Strong scam signal
  • The word "appointment" appears in 25% of legit messages but only 1% of scam ones → Strong legit signal
  • The word "your" appears equally in both → Neutral, not helpful

Step 3: Making a Prediction

🔍
A new message arrives: "HMRC: Claim your refund of £312 at hmrc-refund.uk"


The model checks each word:
"HMRC" → 🚨 Scam signal
"Claim" → 🚨 Scam signal
"refund" → 🚨 Scam signal
".uk link" → 🚨 Scam signal

It multiplies all the probabilities together: P(SCAM | these words) = very high
Decision: SCAM ✅ (83% confidence)

Why "Naive"?

It's called "Naive" because it makes a naive assumption: it treats every word as completely independent. In reality, "tax" and "refund" together mean more than each alone, but Naive Bayes ignores this. Despite being naive, it works surprisingly well for text!

⚠️ The TF-IDF Twist: Before feeding words to Naive Bayes, we transform them using TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency). This gives higher scores to words that are unique and meaningful, and lower scores to common words like "the", "is", "a". Think of it as giving bonus points for rare but important clues.

📐 7. Understanding the Metrics

Here's what each number actually means, explained with real examples from our project:

Accuracy

Definition: "Out of ALL messages tested, what % did we get right?" — Our model got 21 out of 21 correct = 100%. Simple!

Precision

Definition: "Of everything I labelled as SCAM, how many were actually SCAM?" — Our precision is 100%. Every message predicted as fraudulent was confirmed as genuinely fraudulent. Zero false positives were recorded.

Recall (also called Sensitivity)

Definition: "Of all the ACTUAL scam messages, how many did I catch?" — Our recall is 100%. We caught ALL 10 scam messages in the test set. We didn't miss a single one.

F1-Score

Definition: "The balanced average of Precision and Recall." — F1 of 100% means our model strikes a great balance between catching scams and not falsely flagging normal messages.
⚠️ For a scam detector, Recall is the most important metric! It's far worse to MISS a scam (which could cost someone money) than to incorrectly flag a harmless message. So we prioritise high Recall, even if Precision drops slightly.

❓ 12. FAQ — Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is "alpha=1.0" in the code?

This is called Laplace Smoothing. Without it, if a word appears in a test message that was never in the training data, the model would calculate a probability of zero — which would make the entire prediction zero (useless). Alpha=1 adds a tiny count of 1 to every word, even unseen ones, preventing this "zero problem".

Q: Why 80/20 split?

It's the most common convention in machine learning. You want enough data to train the model (80%) but also enough to test it fairly (20%). Evaluating the model on its training data would be equivalent to testing a student on questions they have already seen — it wouldn't tell you if they really learned anything.

Q: Can this be fooled?

Yes! A sophisticated scammer could deliberately avoid trigger words. That's why real fraud systems use many models working together. But for a beginner project demonstrating the concept, this is excellent performance. In the real world, this could be deployed as a first-pass filter.

Q: Why not use a more complex model?

Naive Bayes is ideal for this dataset size and task. More complex models (like neural networks) need far more data and computing power to outperform Naive Bayes on short text classification. A simpler, well-fitted model frequently outperforms a complex one with marginal gain.

Q: What is ngram_range=(1,2)?

This tells TF-IDF to look at individual words (1-grams like "HMRC") AND pairs of words (2-grams like "HMRC tax", "claim now"). The phrase "legal action" is more meaningful than "legal" or "action" alone. Bigrams help capture these important phrase patterns.

Q: What's random_state=42?

This just means "use seed number 42 for the random split". Setting a fixed seed ensures that if you run the code twice, you get the exact same training/test split — making results reproducible. The number 42 is a widely adopted convention in the data science community.

🔒 13. UK GDPR & Data Protection Act 2018 — Compliance

Every data analyst working with message data, personal communications, or text in the UK must understand the legal framework that governs personal data. This section explains UK GDPR in plain terms and how this project was designed to comply with it.

What is UK GDPR?

The UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) is the post-Brexit version of the EU's GDPR, retained into UK law by the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 and supplemented by the Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA 2018). It is enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) — the UK's independent data protection authority.

🏛️ Who enforces it? The ICO (ico.org.uk) is the regulatory body. It can issue fines of up to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover — whichever is higher — for serious breaches. All organisations processing personal data commercially must register with the ICO.

The Six Lawful Bases for Processing Personal Data

Under Article 6 of UK GDPR, you must have a lawful basis before processing personal data. You must identify this basis before you begin processing — you cannot retrospectively decide.

The Seven Data Protection Principles (Article 5 UK GDPR)

All personal data processing must comply with these seven principles:

  1. Lawfulness, Fairness and Transparency — Process data legally, fairly, and openly
  2. Purpose Limitation — Collect data only for specified, explicit, legitimate purposes
  3. Data Minimisation — Collect only what is strictly necessary
  4. Accuracy — Keep data accurate and up to date
  5. Storage Limitation — Do not retain data longer than necessary
  6. Integrity and Confidentiality (Security) — Protect against unauthorised access, loss, or destruction
  7. Accountability — Be able to demonstrate compliance with all the above

How This Project Complies

✅ Synthetic Data — No Personal Data Processed The dataset used in this project consists entirely of synthetic, manually authored messages. No real SMS messages, emails, or personal communications were used. No real individuals are represented. Since no personal data is processed, UK GDPR does not apply to the training dataset itself.

A scam detector — if it resulted in message blocking, account suspension, or access denial — would constitute automated decision-making. In production, this model must operate with human oversight and review. It must not be the sole basis for any significant decision affecting an individual.
